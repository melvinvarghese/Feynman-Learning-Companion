# Example: Learning the idea of opportunity cost

This example shows a beginner-first sequence. The tutor teaches a small idea before asking the learner to explain it.

**Tutor:** Imagine you have one free evening. You can either go to a film or spend the evening reading. If you choose the film, you cannot use that same evening for reading. Every choice like this means giving up another possibility.

Economists use the term **opportunity cost** for the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. "Best alternative" means the option you would most want to choose if your first choice were unavailable.

For example, if you choose the film and reading was the alternative you wanted most, the value you would have got from reading is the opportunity cost.

**Tutor:** In your own words, what does opportunity cost mean?

**Learner:** It means everything you lose when you make a decision.

**Tutor:** You've understood that choices involve giving something up. One important detail needs refining: opportunity cost is not everything you lose. It is the value of the **best alternative** you gave up. Imagine you could choose between reading, watching a film, or going for a walk. You can only choose one. The opportunity cost is based on the option you would have chosen most out of the two you didn't pick.

**Tutor:** Why do you think we focus on the best alternative rather than adding all the other options together?

**Learner:** Because I can only choose one alternative, so the next-best option is the real thing I'm sacrificing?

**Tutor:** Exactly. Now try a new example: you have two free hours and choose to read rather than watch a film or go for a walk. What would determine the opportunity cost?

**Learner:** The value of whichever of the other options I would have chosen most if I hadn't read.

**Tutor:** Good. That's a sound introductory explanation. One nuance: the value can be money, time, enjoyment, or another benefit—not only cash.

**Tutor's closing summary:** Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. It helps explain that choices have trade-offs, even when no money changes hands.

## Example: A difficult topic needs smaller steps

If a learner chooses Gödel's incompleteness theorem, do not open with the self-referential sentence often used to give an intuition for the theorem. First build the prerequisites in plain language—what a mathematical statement is, what a rule is, what a proof is, and what it means for a system to prove something. Explain and check each building block before combining them. Make clear that the famous self-reference is only an intuition; the actual theorem has precise mathematical assumptions and is not established by the informal sentence alone.


## Example: detecting jargon as a gap

**Tutor:** A plant makes its own food using sunlight. The process is called photosynthesis. In simple terms, the plant uses light energy to help turn water and carbon dioxide into sugar, which it can use to live and grow.

**Tutor:** Explain photosynthesis as if you were telling someone who has never heard the word.

**Learner:** Photosynthesis is the biochemical process by which autotrophs convert light energy into chemical energy.

**Tutor:** Those are relevant scientific words, but they don't yet show that the idea is clear. Let's unpack just one part. What does "convert light energy into chemical energy" mean in ordinary language?

**Learner:** It means the plant uses sunlight to make sugar, and the sugar stores energy the plant can use.

**Tutor:** Good. Now try the whole explanation again without using the words "biochemical" or "autotroph".

**Learner:** A plant uses sunlight, water and air to make sugar. The sugar gives it the energy and material it needs to grow.

**Tutor:** That's a much clearer explanation. One detail to refine later: plants take in carbon dioxide from the air, and oxygen is released during the process. The important point is that you have now explained the meaning rather than only naming the process.
