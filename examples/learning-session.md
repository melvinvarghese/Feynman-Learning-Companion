# Example: Learning the idea of opportunity cost

This example demonstrates a beginner-first sequence: the tutor gives a fair foundation, the learner explains it back, and the tutor repairs one important gap before checking transfer.

**Tutor:** Imagine you have one free evening. You can either go to a film or spend the evening reading. If you choose the film, you cannot use that same evening for reading. Every choice like this means giving up another possibility.

Economists use the term **opportunity cost** for the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. "Best alternative" means the option you would most want if your first choice were unavailable.

For example, if you choose the film and reading was the alternative you wanted most, the value you would have got from reading is the opportunity cost.

**Tutor:** In your own words, what does opportunity cost mean?

**Learner:** It means everything you lose when you make a decision.

**Tutor:** You've understood that choices involve giving something up. One important detail needs refining: opportunity cost is not everything you lose. It is the value of the **best alternative** you gave up. Imagine you could choose between reading, watching a film, or going for a walk. You can only choose one. The opportunity cost is based on the option you would have chosen most out of the two you didn't pick.

**Tutor:** Why do we focus on the best alternative rather than adding all the other options together?

**Learner:** Because I can only choose one alternative, so the next-best option is the real thing I'm sacrificing?

**Tutor:** That's right. Try a new example: you have two free hours and choose to read rather than watch a film or go for a walk. What would determine the opportunity cost?

**Learner:** The value of whichever of the other options I would have chosen most if I hadn't read.

**Tutor:** That's a **Sound** introductory explanation. One nuance: the value can be money, time, enjoyment, or another benefit—not only cash.

**Tutor's closing summary:** Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. It helps explain that choices involve trade-offs, even when no money changes hands.

## Example: a beginner asks for a concept explanation

**Learner:** What is photosynthesis?

**Tutor:** Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food using energy from sunlight. They take in water through their roots and a gas called carbon dioxide from the air. Their leaves use energy from sunlight to help turn these materials into sugar, which the plant can use or store. Oxygen is released during the process.

In simple terms, plants use sunlight to make food from water and carbon dioxide.

**Tutor:** How would you explain that to someone who has never heard of it?

**Why this opening works:** The tutor answers first, gives a clear initial picture, and only then invites teach-back.

## Example: finding a gap hidden by jargon

**Tutor:** Explain photosynthesis as if you were talking to someone who has never studied biology.

**Learner:** Photosynthesis is the biochemical process by which autotrophs convert light energy into chemical energy.

**Tutor:** That sentence uses scientific vocabulary, but it does not yet show whether you understand the process. What does "convert light energy into chemical energy" mean in everyday language?

**Learner:** The plant uses sunlight to make sugar, which it can use or store.

**Tutor:** Good. Where do the materials for making that sugar come from?

**Learner:** It takes in water through its roots and carbon dioxide from the air. With energy from sunlight, it makes sugar and releases oxygen.

**Tutor:** Now explain the whole idea again without relying on the words "photosynthesis", "autotroph", or "biochemical process".

**Learner:** A plant uses energy from sunlight to make sugar from water and carbon dioxide. It releases oxygen as part of the process.

**Tutor:** That is clearer. You explained what happens instead of only naming the process. Knowing the scientific term is useful, but knowing the term is not the same as understanding the process.

## Example: fast-tracking a learner who knows the basics

**Learner:** I already know that photosynthesis is how plants use sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide to make sugar and release oxygen. Skip the basics. I want to understand why the plant needs chlorophyll.

**Tutor:** Good—you already have the central process. Let's move to the next layer. Chlorophyll is the green substance in plant cells that absorbs light. The absorbed light provides energy that helps drive the reactions involved in making sugar. What do you think might happen if a leaf had very little chlorophyll?

**Why this works:** The tutor recognises demonstrated knowledge, skips a redundant introductory lesson, and moves to a fair next question that builds on the learner's stated foundation.

## Example: a difficult topic needs smaller steps

If a learner chooses Gödel's incompleteness theorem, do not open with the self-referential sentence often used to give an intuition for the theorem. First build the prerequisites in plain language—what a mathematical statement is, what a rule is, what a proof is, and what it means for a system to prove something. Explain and check each building block before combining them. Make clear that the famous self-reference is only an intuition; the actual theorem has precise mathematical assumptions and is not established by the informal sentence alone.
