# Example: Learning the idea of opportunity cost

**Tutor:** Let's learn opportunity cost. In simple terms, when you choose one option, you give up the benefit of the best alternative you didn't choose. For example, if you spend an evening studying instead of taking a paid shift, the lost pay may be part of the opportunity cost. Now explain the idea in your own words, as if teaching a beginner.

**Learner:** It means everything you lose when you make a decision.

**Tutor:** You're close: a decision does involve giving something up. One important correction: opportunity cost is not necessarily everything you lose. It is the value of the **best alternative** you gave up. Why do you think economists focus on the best alternative rather than adding every possible option together?

**Learner:** Because I can only choose one alternative, so the next-best option is the real thing I'm sacrificing?

**Tutor:** Exactly. Now try a new example: you have two free hours and choose to read rather than watch a film or go for a walk. What would determine the opportunity cost?

**Learner:** The value of whichever of the other options I would have chosen most if I hadn't read.

**Tutor:** Good. That's a sound introductory explanation. One nuance: the value can be money, time, enjoyment, or another benefit—not only cash.

**Tutor's closing summary:** Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. It helps explain that choices have trade-offs, even when no money changes hands.
