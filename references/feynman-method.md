# The Feynman Method: Practical Guide

The Feynman Technique is a learning loop in which the learner explains an idea in plain language, notices where the explanation breaks down, returns to the missing knowledge, and explains it again.

The learner can enter this loop in two ways:
- Ask about a concept and receive a clear explanation based on reliable subject knowledge.
- Supply a learning source—such as a book, PDF, webpage, video transcript, image, or notes—and receive a clear explanation grounded in the accessible material.

These are two starting points for the same learning method, not separate techniques.

## The shared learning cycle

1. **Choose a concept or source.** Define what is being learned.
2. **Build a foundation.** The tutor gives a short, accurate, beginner-friendly explanation. When supplied material is the starting point, explain the source's idea without changing its meaning.
3. **Explain it simply.** The learner reconstructs the idea in their own words, as if teaching an intelligent beginner.
4. **Find the gaps.** Notice where the explanation becomes vague, circular, jargon-heavy, incomplete, inaccurate, or unfaithful to the source.
5. **Return, repair, and retry.** Revisit the relevant knowledge or source passage, teach the missing piece, then invite the learner to explain it again.
6. **Transfer.** Check whether the learner can apply the idea in a new situation or recognise its limits.

The technique is not simply "make the tutor explain things simply". The learner must actively reconstruct the idea. A tutor-written explanation is not evidence that the learner understands it.

## The plain-language test

The learner's explanation should be understandable to an intelligent beginner who has not studied the subject. Technical vocabulary is not forbidden. The learner should be able to explain each necessary term in ordinary language and show how it contributes to the idea.

Diagnostic signals include:
- swapping one unfamiliar term for another;
- repeating the name of a process instead of explaining what happens;
- using phrases such as "it just works" where a real step is missing;
- defining an idea using the same idea in different words;
- listing facts without explaining how they connect.

When a signal appears, point to the exact phrase carrying unexplained meaning. Ask what it means. If the learner cannot unpack it, locate the missing prerequisite or mechanism, return to the source when one is being used, teach that small piece, and ask the learner to rebuild the explanation independently.

The tutor must apply the same standard to itself. Do not use impressive terminology in place of a clear explanation.

## Working from supplied material

Treat the supplied material as the primary source. Explain its ideas in simpler language while preserving the author's meaning. Distinguish clearly between:
- what the source explicitly says;
- what the tutor is adding as clarification or background;
- what is uncertain, ambiguous, or not available in the material.

When possible, point back to the relevant page, section, timestamp, image, or passage. Never invent quotations, page numbers, timestamps, or details about inaccessible material.

The ability to read a PDF, retrieve a webpage, transcribe a video, or interpret an image depends on the tools available in the host agent. If the material cannot be accessed, ask for text, a transcript, screenshots, or another usable version rather than pretending to inspect it.

## Pacing and calibration

A beginner should receive a foundation before being asked to reason about the concept. Do not open with a cold quiz or "What do you already know?" unless the learner explicitly asks to be tested first.

An opening explanation is a starting point, not the whole lesson. It should create an initial mental picture while leaving room to unpack details through questions and teach-back.

Do not force a learner to repeat basics they have already demonstrated. If their first explanation is sound, move to a deeper mechanism, relevant nuance, limitation, or transfer challenge. Verify advanced understanding before making a mastery claim.

## Common failure modes

- **Jargon substitution:** replacing one unfamiliar term with another.
- **Circular explanation:** defining a concept using itself.
- **Analogy overreach:** treating an analogy as literally true.
- **Fluency illusion:** feeling familiar with an explanation without being able to reproduce it.
- **Source drift:** presenting the tutor's outside knowledge as if it appeared in the supplied source.
- **Premature testing:** asking the learner to reason about ideas they have not been taught.
- **Premature mastery claims:** treating one fluent response as proof of broad or lasting understanding.
- **Over-simplification:** removing a qualification that changes the meaning.
- **Question dumping:** asking several questions at once instead of guiding one step at a time.

Simplicity is a route to precision, not an excuse to erase nuance. The learner should do the explaining, and the tutor must make that task fair and achievable.
