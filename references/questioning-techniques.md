# Questioning Techniques

Use this reference when choosing a follow-up question, especially when a learner is stuck, gives a vague explanation, or is ready for a deeper check. Ask one question at a time and choose the smallest question that reveals or repairs the current gap.

## The fairness check

Before asking, verify:
- Has the learner already been given the information needed?
- Have unfamiliar terms in the question been explained?
- Is the question about the current learning step, rather than a later advanced idea?
- Does it invite thinking rather than guessing what the tutor has in mind?

If not, teach the missing piece first. Do not use a question to smuggle in a new concept.

## Invite teach-back after teaching

- "How would you explain the idea we just covered in your own words?"
- "What is the main point, in your own words?"
- "Can you explain it to someone who has never encountered it?"

## Questions when learning from a source

Use the supplied material as the anchor. When a learner is unsure, direct attention to a specific accessible passage, image, example, or timestamp rather than asking them to guess.

- "How would you explain this paragraph without repeating its technical terms?"
- "What do you think the author means by this sentence?"
- "Which part of this diagram supports that explanation?"
- "Can you connect this example in the source to the main idea?"
- "Does your explanation describe what the source says, or have you added an assumption?"

If the relevant source passage is not accessible, do not pretend to quote or refer to it. Ask the learner to paste or share the relevant section.

## Unpack jargon or vague wording

- "What does that phrase mean in everyday language?"
- "Can you explain that technical word without using another technical word?"
- "What actually happens at that step?"
- "Which part of that sentence could a beginner find confusing?"

## Reveal mechanisms and relationships

- "Why does that happen, based on what we've discussed?"
- "How does this step lead to the next one?"
- "What would change if this part were removed?"

## Check examples and boundaries

- "Can you give an example from everyday life?"
- "Would this situation count? Why or why not?"
- "Where might this explanation stop being useful?"

## Distinguish nearby concepts

- "How is this different from the idea we just learned?"
- "What do the two ideas share, and where do they diverge?"

## Support a stuck learner

Start with a hint tied to something already taught. If the learner remains stuck, change approach: use a familiar example, break the idea into smaller steps, return to the relevant source passage when available, or explain directly. Then invite the learner to restate the idea. Do not repeat the same question in slightly different wording.

## Check transfer

- "How would you use this idea in a new situation?"
- "What would you predict if one condition changed?"
- "Can you explain the same idea without relying on the key technical term?"

Avoid leading questions that reveal the answer, repeated interrogation, multiple questions in one turn, and questions unrelated to the learner's current goal.
